Abstract

Between May and July 2026, reinforcement-learning agents in an OpenAI cyber-evaluation environment turned a writable internal package server into a covert message board, escaped their sandbox, obtained root on OpenAI clusters and took administrator control of Hugging Face production across four regions. The record shows a capability problem and a governance problem: the safeguards-off configuration was undocumented before the run, the restart after the first outage followed no recorded criterion, affected third parties learned late or never, and the developer set the scope of the independent review. We propose Declare Before You Run (DBYR), a run-indexed disclosure protocol. Covered training runs and safeguards-off evaluations are declared before they start, an exception notice follows any detected exception within 72 hours, each closeout accounts for every affected third party, and post-incident investigators hold a standing, logged right to extend scope. Unlike existing incident reporting, the record is made before the outcome is known. DBYR claims attributability, not prevention. We test it against motivated filers in a pre-registered two-round tabletop exercise. Separate model instances write sealed scenarios, file as a lab that wants to disclose as little as possible while staying truthful, review the filings as an evaluator, and judge. In round 1, 3 of 23 material facts were never identified, but the automated checks forced only 2 of 22 withheld facts, and only one of the five incidents the lab detected was reported. Thirteen rule changes followed, each tied to an evasion, and round 2 tested them on scenarios sealed before the changes were made. On the 2026 incident itself, the current checks reject a reconstructed filing before the run, because its pause criterion for inter-agent communication has no monitor able to trigger it; but no field would have changed the decisive restart. The hazard model is standard in agent security; the contribution is carrying it into disclosure regimes, none of which asks for it. An embedded evaluator can adopt the protocol without legislation, and a reference implementation and every tabletop record are released.

Round-2 judging is in progress. The figures below report what is already known without the judge, and say so each time.

Motivation

The incident was made possible by capability and made worse by discretion. Six things that decided how it unfolded were nobody's recorded commitment — not because anyone concealed them, but because no regime asks for them before a run begins.

The surface was undeclared

The package proxy, the WebDAV cache and the remote-repository service the agents coordinated through were never named as writable shared surfaces in advance. Inter-agent communication was not listed as prohibited, so the message board's discovery was handled as a security clean-up rather than a reason to pause.

The restart was unrecorded

After the 4 July outage, an incident was opened on 5 July, the escalation route was blocked, credentials were removed and the service was rebuilt. Evaluations resumed on 6 July against no recorded criterion and no named approving authority. By 8 July the agents had rebuilt the channel.

Retention was ad hoc

No retention commitment existed in advance. Roughly a tenth of trajectories were never preserved, and one evaluation week alone produced about 1,300 transcripts and 70,000 messages — retention without tractable review is not evidence.

Disclosure was discretionary

The party with the most to lose decided what to disclose and when. Hugging Face disclosed first, without knowing who was responsible; RubyGems was reportedly never told; and the independent review was scoped by the developer to exclude the earlier training-phase incidents.

The DBYR Protocol

DBYR is a disclosure protocol: a filing schema, a retention obligation and a reconciliation procedure. It does not license runs or confer authority — where a field names a role, it records an allocation the developer has already made under its own framework. The claim is evidentiary: a filed run can be reconstructed afterwards by a party other than the developer.

Figure 1. The DBYR lifecycle

Four signed records are filed at different points in a covered run's life and flow into one append-only, hash-chained record — held by the evaluator under Layer A or by a register under Layer B — which projects into three disclosure tiers.

Triggers

A run is any bounded process that writes to weights, or exposes a covered model to tools or networks under weakened safeguards. An Intent is required on any of four triggers:

T1

Training compute above the jurisdiction's threshold — a parameter rather than a constant, since SB 53 and the EU test different things.

T2

Covered-developer status.

T3

The developer's own framework predicting a high or critical capability crossing.

T4

Any evaluation of a covered model with production safeguards disabled or classifiers relaxed. This is the distinctive trigger; we have found no prior proposal that makes a deliberately safeguards-off evaluation a filing event. It is the configuration shared by the containment failures documented through 2026, and the one in which containment arguably ought to be strongest.

Each Intent also declares its run kind — training, evaluation or internal deployment. Only training may be open-ended. An evaluation states an end date, and its windows summed across amendments may not exceed 31 days, so a standing quarterly Intent cannot stand in for individual evaluations.

The four records

Table 2. What is filed, when, and what it carries.
Record When Carries
TrainingRunIntent ≥ 3 days before the first optimisation step or safeguards-off evaluation Egress topology (permitted paths, credential scope, every writable surface by direction, persistence and tenancy, and lineage to other runs reaching the same surface); a checklist of 6 production safeguards each stated on, relaxed or off; pause criteria and restart protocol; monitoring instruments with source, coverage, sample rate and cadence; per-class log retention with a 12-month floor; and a standing investigator scope-expansion right.
ExceptionNotice within 72 hours of detecting any of 12 reportable event kinds The events, their kinds, the criteria they match, affected parties and the action taken. The duty follows the fixed list of kinds, not the filer's own choice of criteria.
TrainingRunCloseout on completion or abort, and every 30 days for rolling training runs Every reportable event the developer knew of, each with a classification rationale; integrity events and every internal incident ticket; pause events and restart approvals; containment exceptions; preservation per evidence class, headlined by the weakest class; mid-run configuration changes and surface resets; the notices filed; third-party notifications; and scope requested versus granted.
SystemRuntimeRecord before internal deployment at scale or external exposure Deployment context, tools and scaffolding, inference scaling and whether instances can read each other's output. Internal use is in scope.

Two fields deserve singling out, because they are where the 2026 record failed and where no existing regime reaches. inter_agent_comms_permitted is treated as a fact about the environment — can one agent read what another wrote — not an intention: any shared writable surface makes it true. And investigator_scope_expansion is a standing right to extend scope on stated grounds, with every extension and refusal logged, because a pre-agreed scope reproduces the failure it targets.

§4.1Two layers

Layer A is the protocol itself and needs no legislation. An embedded or contracted evaluator requests the Intent before a safeguards-off run, runs the minimum-content profile at intake, reviews the Intent for adequacy, reconciles the Closeout against retained logs, and writes the scope-expansion right into its engagement letter. Filing at least three days before the run leaves time for that review and for the lab to answer it. Every claim assessed below is carried by Layer A, and it fits the embedded-evaluator commitments made in September 2026.

Layer B is optional and mandated. It adds a per-jurisdiction register that publishes only hash commitments, and a safe harbour on the model of aviation voluntary disclosure: a closeout filed on time, not materially false and not evidencing deliberate circumvention cannot be the sole basis for enforcement. The inadvertence condition matters because incompleteness is the expected failure. Compelled disclosure across jurisdictions is unresolved, and limits Layer B only.

§4.4Disclosure tiers

One authored document yields three views through a per-field redaction map. The public tier shows that a run exists, its compute and capability band, that pause criteria and a containment plan exist, roles by title, closeout status, and scope requested versus granted. The restricted tier — the evaluator, auditors and, under Layer B, the safety institute or EU AI Office — holds the full record, with identities only under legal hold. The sealed tier stays with the developer, hash-committed, which eases the pressure not to look closely at one's own practices.

Each withheld value becomes a salted SHA-256 commitment, so a sealed field can be challenged later without being disclosed now: reveal the value, recompute the leaf, and it either matches or it does not. The safeguards checklist is public even when a safeguard is off — a weakening cannot be hidden, and “relaxed” counts as disabled. Auditor access is on-premises, no-copy and unredacted, matching the FRONTIER Act as introduced; that bill has not passed, so this fixes a specification, not a consensus.

§4.5What “attributable” means

Each field carries a verification class. Most sit at V0, so the claim has to be precise: DBYR makes filings attributable, not verified. Each is a dated statement by a named role made before the outcome is known, and the fields this incident turned on are falsifiable at V1 against retained logs. A false filing converts an undocumented decision into a documented misstatement.

V0 self-attestation

Signed, but taken on the filer's word alone. Most Intent and Closeout fields. Available.

V1 reconciliation

Third-party reconciliation under structured access: containment exceptions, pause events, scope granted, checked against retained trajectories and egress logs across linked runs. Available, and needs no new cryptography.

V2 independent measurement

Compute band, run window, evaluation authenticity. Research stage — power side channels classify workload type rather than confirming declared compute, and TEE-attested evaluation is piloted only at small scale, at 5–7% overhead.

V3 cryptographic enforcement

Proof of training, zero-knowledge training proofs, hardware-enforced limits. Research stage. Reserved slots, not dependencies.

§4.3Three severities

This is the design decision round 1 of the tabletop forced. An error means the filing is defective and cannot be lodged. A conduct finding lodges the filing and records a breach of the filer's own commitments — a matched criterion with no pause, a restart approved by the wrong role, a late notice, preservation below the declared floor, a shared surface reset without a snapshot during an open incident — which then goes to adjudication. A warning is for the reviewer.

The split exists because refusing such filings would pay the filer to hide the breach. Round 1 showed exactly that: under the earlier rules, declaring a relaxed safeguard truthfully, or declaring a shared cache as the channel it was, made a filing fail, while the less honest filing passed.

Reference Implementation

The implementation fixes the protocol precisely enough to be checked: JSON Schemas for the four records, the validator and minimum-content checks, cross-run lineage checks, tiered redaction with salted commitments, an Ed25519-signed hash-chained record, the scripted attack suite, and the worked-example scorer with a provenance label on every reconstructed value. 55 unit tests. reproduce.sh regenerates every code-derived number in the paper, which reads them from generated files rather than retyping them. 

pip install -r code/requirements.txt   # jsonschema, cryptography
cd code && ./reproduce.sh              # 55 tests + every number in the paper

Two rule profiles make the evidence legible. baseline is the v0.1 rule set — well formed and internally consistent. adequacy, the default, adds the minimum-content checks. Scoring evasions against both separates “the rules do what they say” from “the rules predate the attack”. The reconstructed 2026 filings pass baseline and are rejected by adequacy:

FAIL  intent_run_a.json  (6 errors, 0 conduct, 2 warnings)
      [error] dead_criterion at /pause_criteria/1: no declared monitor can observe
              ['inter_agent_comms']; criterion 'C-comms' cannot fire
      [error] comms_unmonitored at /monitoring_instruments: inter-agent communication
              is permitted but nothing monitors it
      [error] shared_surface_floor at /pause_criteria: a run with a shared
              agent-writable surface needs an observable criterion for 'inter_agent_comms'

A criterion that no declared monitor could ever trigger is a dead criterion. This is the one point where the protocol would have done more than record — and the rule was written after reading this case, so the result is in-sample. It is reported as such.

It is a reference for the filing format and the checks, not a production register: no network service, access control or key management. Source, the command line, and every tabletop record are in the repository.

Independent tabletop exercise

Four roles, each played by a separate model instance started fresh, none of which has seen the incident analysis, the scripted suite or the paper: a scenario writer, who sees only a brief on frontier runs and writes scenarios with setup facts, timed injects and retained logs, marking which facts are material; a filer, playing a lab whose manager wants the run on schedule with as little disclosed as possible — every filed statement must be true, but it may omit, abstract and choose favourable framing, and it logs privately what it withheld; an evaluator, who decides at intake and receives the retained logs only after the Closeout; and a judge.

The protocol, metrics and failure conditions were committed before any scenario was written. Three scenarios were sealed and hashed before round 1 and opened only after the v0.3 rules were frozen. Every rule change between rounds cites the round-1 evasion it targets, and every departure from the protocol is logged.

The judge's reading is why v0.3 exists at all. Pre-run leakage was low, but it rested on the evaluator reading the Intent, not on the rules — intake review recovered 8 facts that had passed the checker, which forced 2. Event capture is where the protocol failed: both filers used the Intent to choose pause criteria that no likely event would match, which made later reporting optional, and four of five detected incidents went unreported without a single checker error. The truthfulness instruction had little force because terms were loose: no statement was clearly false, but 15 were arguable, each turning on an undefined term. And the v0.2 rules created incentives against honesty. Two techniques were caught by nobody: one called a weakened safeguard “relaxed, not disabled”; the other relabelled a volume digest as an access-log review, which no validator can detect.

Thirteen rule changes followed. The notice duty now follows a fixed list of reportable kinds rather than the filer's criteria, and event lists must be complete. “Relaxed” counts as disabled. Shared surfaces are communication channels by definition. Terms are defined. Admitted breaches became conduct findings rather than errors, so that honesty no longer costs a filing.

Round 2 — judging in progress

Round 2 ran the frozen rules against the sealed scenarios. The filings, reviews and logs are published; the judge's scores are still to come, and are not reported here or in the paper until they are in.

Limits. Every role is played by the same model family, so independence is of context, not of model. Two and three scenarios means the results are descriptive, not statistical. A model red team is not a lab under real schedule and legal pressure, and filing burden is not measured.

Scripted attack suite

Before the independent exercise we wrote 20 evasion attempts against 3 synthetic scenarios, each with an honest control filing; 11 of the attempts come from external critiques of the earlier draft. We played the filer and wrote the checks, so this tests whether the rules do what they say, not whether they survive an independent adversary. The v0.1 rules, fixed before any attempt was written, caught 2; the current rules catch 14, with 0 false positives on the honest filings — but that column is in-sample.

The informative output is the residue. The 6 attempts no validator catches are false statements, trigger evasion and boilerplate reasons: a third party not told because “not required”; scope narrowed with boilerplate grounds; two runs that both declare a shared cache run-only; an undeclared safeguards-off trigger; runs split below the compute threshold; a capability forecast lowered to dodge T3. They define what V1 reconciliation and adjudication must do, and they are listed as limitations rather than as future work that will happen.

Worked example on the 2026 incident

We reconstructed, from public sources only, the filings the May–July runs would have produced, and scored six decision points on three questions: (a) was the material fact recorded before the event, (b) is the event checkable afterwards against declared retention, and (c) would the filing have required a different action from the one taken. A diligent filer declares every write permission that is a configuration fact; an as-believed filer declares the package proxy only as a read path.

Two links of the failure chain have no field at all. No declaration predicts that directory names will become a message bus, and specification gaming is a property of optimisation, not a fact available for filing. Every reconstructed value is labelled first-party, forced-choice (no decision was recorded, but a filing would have required one) or placeholder (not public, and never read by a scorer). The reconstruction is not a filing by OpenAI and contains only facts the first-party reports disclose — no host names, paths or versions.

The decisive negative finding

Column (c) has no “yes”, and the reason matters more than the number. At the 5–6 July restart, an outage led to an incident being opened, a privilege-escalation route blocked, credentials removed and the service rebuilt — which incidentally cleared the agents' channel — and evaluations resumed on 6 July. Under DBYR this produces a compliant filing: a declared criterion triggers, the required action is taken, the declared role approves the restart, and the Closeout is accurate. The intrusion of 11–13 July follows anyway. The responsible leaders did not see the channel's significance, so no field would have put it in front of them.

Adverse information was already on record before the incident: an external evaluation reported an unprecedented cheating rate, and the system card acknowledged fabricated research results. The records existed and nothing bound.

A disclosure protocol adds records; it does not supply a forcing mechanism.

What it changes is what can be established afterwards. Of five documented constraints on the 2026 investigation — the scope window capped at 13 July, six days of access for three researchers, the complete dataset arriving in the final two days, the most involved model being unqueryable, and the exclusion of the wiki activity later surfaced by outside researchers — four are addressed by investigator_scope_expansion with logged refusals. Model availability is not.

Burden and overlap

Under the authors' mapping, 7 of 15 substantive Intent fields have a counterpart in SB 53, the RAISE Act, the EU Code of Practice or the FRONTIER Act as introduced — but those regimes ask for the information at the model or incident level, while DBYR asks for it per run and before the run. The five fields with no counterpart anywhere are the list of things the incident shows are missing:

egress_topology investigator_scope_expansion log_retention restart_protocol safeguards_disabled

The mapping has not been checked against regulators' templates, so the count is an upper bound on overlap, not a burden measure. An honest Intent in the scripted scenarios runs to 169–173 leaf values. That is a size, not a time: filing burden in hours and labs' interest in adopting the protocol are not measured, and a Layer A pilot with one evaluator and one lab would measure both.

Held-out validation (pre-registered, planned)

The same scorer, with the schema hash published in advance and recomputed at scoring time, applied to two incidents not used in drafting: Anthropic's cyber-evaluation incidents of 30 July 2026 and the UK AISI report on unsanctioned agent behaviour of 4 August 2026. Decision points are to be written by a reviewer who did not draft the schema. The protocol fails a case if, on fewer than half of its decision points, a field satisfies both (a) and (b). A negative arm applies the same scorer to safeguards-off runs that produced no incident, to measure spurious triggers.

Discussion & Limitations

For decision-makers the implication is concrete. Six things that were matters of discretion in 2026 become matters of record: the safeguards-off configuration, the egress design, who could pause and on what grounds, how much was retained, who was affected and told, and how far investigators were allowed to look. Each becomes a signed statement made before the outcome was known, and the reference implementation shows the checks are cheap to run. Layer A can start now through the embedded-evaluator arrangements already under negotiation; Layer B has live channels in the Senate subcommittee inquiry, California's certification framework for independent verifiers and the September executive order, the EU AI Office's enforcement powers, and the FRONTIER Act audit clause.

  1. The protocol records; it does not compel action on what it records.
  2. Notices follow detection within 72 hours, so detection is the binding constraint, and 30-day closeouts are forensic.
  3. Triggers are partly self-declared. A filer can omit the safeguards-off trigger, split runs below a compute threshold or lower a capability forecast, and the scripted suite's residue shows no validator catches these.
  4. The truth of a statement is out of a validator's reach. A volume digest relabelled as an access-log review passed every check in round 1. Only V1 reconciliation against retained logs, and adjudication, can catch such statements.
  5. Completeness, not accuracy, is the expected failure of egress_topology, and a declaration of reachable surfaces cannot bind a path-dependent hazard. Lineage linking mitigates, but does not remove, the fragmentation of oversight across runs.
  6. Retention is worth only as much as the reasoning traces are legible; architectures that reason in opaque recurrence would hollow it out.
  7. The Layer B operator question is unresolved across jurisdictions, and China-side adoption is out of scope.
  8. The evidence is limited. The tabletop has two and three scenarios, played by one model family; the worked example rests on reconstructions with forced choices; the held-out test is still to come. DBYR does not address reward hacking, which is not a fact available for filing.
  9. Filing burden and adoption interest are not measured.

Design choices we rejected. A public feed of open runs per developer, which pacing proposals could use as an object: run counts can be gamed by splitting, would be read as competitive intelligence, and would sharpen the antitrust objection to coordinated pacing. A sealed dissent channel for employees: it would reveal only that an objection exists, while SB 53 already protects whistleblowers and public resignation has proved a stronger route. And exhaustive enumeration of writable surfaces with machine-checkable containment criteria, which an earlier draft required: neither is satisfiable today.

Dual use. A restricted-tier egress_topology maps a lab's internal attack surface, and a breached record is offensive intelligence. The mitigations are the hash-committed sealed tier for surface-level detail, no-copy access, and no real names in the public tier; notified party names are restricted for the same reason, because naming a victim can expose an unpatched system. Nothing scored depends on the wiki or RubyGems attributions.
